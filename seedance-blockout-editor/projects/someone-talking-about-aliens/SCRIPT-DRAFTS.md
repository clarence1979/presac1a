# Script drafts: someone talking about aliens (30 s, one take, documentary)

Written 14 Nov 2026 for the approved 9-beat story (man in frame the whole time). Read-only context: PLAN.md, beats.json, QA-BUILD.md, stills t012.2 / t014.9 / t027.4.

Note: the current blockout (beats.json) is the older 8-beat order (dog, dish, man, photos, point, neighbours, saucer). These scripts follow the new 9-beat structure below, so the blockout will need re-timing to match.

| Beat | Time | Picture |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0-3 | leans into the lens, hands shape a disc |
| 2 | 3-6.5 | slaps down photos of lights in the sky |
| 3 | 6.5-10 | walks off the porch beckoning us, dog at heel |
| 4 | 10-13.5 | points up; sky empty; arms drop |
| 5 | 13.5-17 | behind him the dish swings skyward, dog barks; he keeps talking |
| 6 | 17-20.5 | neighbours on the road stop and stare past him, a kid points |
| 7 | 20.5-24.5 | saucer slides over the roof behind him, shadow crosses the yard |
| 8 | 24.5-28 | shadow hits him, he turns, cap falls (slow motion, NO speech) |
| 9 | 28-30 | turns back to the lens, payoff line |

**How words are counted:** every spoken word, with "fifty-one" or "ten forty" counted as two. Words/sec = words ÷ (end − start).
**The ceiling:** about 24.8 s can carry speech (beat 8 is silent, with short breaths between lines). At 2.5 words/sec that is about 62 words, and at the 2.8 limit it is 69. Every version sits at 60-62 words, with no line above 2.8 words/sec. More words would mean rushing an old man, and he would stop sounding like one.

**The engine in all three:** he builds his case to us while the proof arrives behind him. Each line is written so that it reads one way to him and the opposite way to the audience, who can see the dish, the dog, the neighbours and the saucer.

## Version A: grounded and sincere ("Tell Ruthie I'm stayin'")

**WALT PRUITT, 74.** Retired well-driller in high-desert New Mexico, widower, slow vowels, drops his g's. He has fifty-one years on this ground.
**Verbal habit:** he gives exact times and dates the way a driller logs depth, as if precision will make him believable.
**What he loses:** his daughter Ruthie thinks the lights are a side effect of his new pills and has found him a room in Albuquerque. If nobody believes him, he loses the house.

## Version B: dry comic ("That's Exhibit D")

**DEL HASKINS, 68.** Goat man from far West Texas, flat as the land, never raises his voice.
**Verbal habit:** he talks like a courtroom, with "exhibits", "granted", and himself in the third person when he is trying to sound official.
**What he loses:** after thirty years of reports, the sheriff will start billing him for every call. He has spent his credibility, and now it costs money.

## Version C: boldest ("Feed the dog")

**AMOS TATE, 81.** Rancher, widower, gravel voice, high-plains New Mexico. He says less than he means.
**Verbal habit:** he makes lists. When he is moved, he counts things instead of saying how he feels.
**What he loses:** nothing, and that is the twist. His wife June died; the lights come at her bedtime; and he is not trying to prove anything. He wants to be taken. The irony turns over: the man begging them to come is the only person who misses them arriving.

## Recommendation

I recommend **C**. The other two use the dramatic irony as a gag, a man who can't see the proof. C makes the irony cut: the one man begging to be taken is the only person who doesn't see them come, so "Feed the dog" lands as a laugh and a goodbye in under two seconds. If C feels too heavy for the channel, **A** is the safe fallback: it is the most natural documentary voice, and "Tell Ruthie I'm stayin'" pays off a stake we planted.
