B Learn the key words 2–6 min

Why are we learning this?

Before a real software team builds anything (a game studio, a bank app, the SEQTA you use every day), they write down exactly what the software must do and how well it must do it. That document is the Software Requirements Specification (SRS). When the software is finished, testers check it against the SRS. That check is the Evaluation stage, and it is exactly what you are doing today.

In Units 3 and 4 Software Development, your School-assessed Task (SAT) asks you to write your own SRS and, later, to evaluate your own software against it. Today you practise on someone else’s game first.

In Evaluation, you look back at the Analysis (the SRS) and judge the finished product against it.

SRS Software Requirements Specification

The document written in the Analysis stage. It describes the purpose of the software and the environment it will be used in. It lists the functional and non-functional requirements, the constraints, the scope, the user characteristics and the technical environment.

Afterglow example: The Afterglow SRS you are using today.

Functional requirement what the software must DO

An action the software must perform: its inputs, processing and outputs. Clue: look for an action (press, open, show, save).

Afterglow example: “Pressing M must open a city map.”

Non-functional requirement how WELL it must do it

A quality the software must have, such as usability, reliability, robustness, portability and maintainability. Clue: look for a quality (easy, fast, reliable) and, ideally, a number you can measure.

Afterglow example: “The game must run at 30 frames per second or more.”

Scope how FAR the solution goes

The boundaries of the solution: what it will do and what it will not do in this version.

Afterglow example: “Version 1.0 has no online multiplayer.”

Constraint what LIMITS the developer

A condition or limitation the developer must work within. The five types are economic (cost, time), technical (speed of processing, capacity, equipment, compatibility, security), social (the users’ level of expertise), legal (intellectual property, ownership and privacy of data) and usability (accessibility, usefulness, ease of use).

Afterglow example: “There is no money to hire 3D artists.”

Assumption what we BELIEVE is true

Something the developer believes is true but has not checked, or cannot control. If an assumption turns out to be wrong, the software may not work for some users.

Afterglow example: “We assume players can hear the game.”

Evaluation did it MEET the SRS?

The last stage of the problem-solving methodology: judging how well the finished software met its requirements, in terms of efficiency (time, cost and effort) and effectiveness (how well it does its job).

Afterglow example: “FR4 was met: Juno spoke to me when I pressed E.”

Memory hook: Functional = what it DOES. Non-functional = how WELL. Scope = how FAR. Constraint = what LIMITS. Assumption = what we BELIEVE. Evaluation = did it MEET the SRS?

Afterglow: Nova Vale Street Racing
Software Requirements Specification, version 1.0 (class summary)

1. Purpose

A free, browser-based 3D street-racing game. Players explore the night-time city of Nova Vale on foot, talk to characters, drive, enter street races and spend their winnings on food, clothes and car upgrades.

2. User characteristics

Secondary students and adults. Some have never played a driving game; others are experienced gamers. Most play on a school or home laptop; some play on a phone or tablet.

3. Technical environment

Runs in a modern web browser (Chrome, Edge, Safari or Firefox) that can show 3D graphics (WebGL). Hosted online, so there is nothing to install. Works with keyboard and mouse, a gamepad, or touch controls. Progress is saved in the browser.

4. Scope

In scope (version 1.0)
  • Single-player play in one fictional city, Nova Vale
  • Walking, running and driving
  • Three street races against computer-controlled rivals
  • Talking to characters
  • Food stalls, shops, a garage, a bank and an apartment
  • Saving progress in the browser or to a file
  • Keyboard and mouse, gamepad and touch controls
Out of scope (not in version 1.0)
  • Online multiplayer or chat with other players
  • Real car brands, real logos or real-world places
  • Spending real money
  • Police chases
  • An app-store app (it runs in the browser only)

5. Constraints

  • Economic (cost, time): There is no budget to hire 3D artists, sound designers or musicians, and the game must be free to play.
  • Technical (speed of processing, capacity, equipment, compatibility, security): The game must run in a web browser on ordinary school laptops that do not have a gaming graphics card.
  • Social (the users’ level of expertise): Players range from people who have never played a driving game to experienced gamers.
  • Legal (intellectual property, ownership and privacy of data): The game must not use real car brands, logos or other people’s artwork without permission, and must not collect players’ personal information.
  • Usability (accessibility, usefulness, ease of use): Players with different needs, devices and preferences must be able to play comfortably.

6. Assumptions

  • A1 We assume players have a keyboard and mouse, a gamepad, or a touch screen.
  • A2 We assume players’ devices and browsers can show 3D graphics (WebGL).
  • A3 We assume players can hear the game through headphones or speakers.
  • A4 We assume the player’s network (home or school) lets them open the game’s website.
  • A5 We assume players can read English.

7. Functional requirements

  • FR1 The game must show a main menu with five options: Hit the streets, How to play, Save or load a game, Settings and Credits.
  • FR2 The player must be able to walk (W A S D), run (hold Shift) and look around (drag the mouse) on foot.
  • FR3 Pressing M must open a city map. Clicking a place must set a waypoint that guides the player there.
  • FR4 Pressing E when close to a character must start a conversation with them.
  • FR5 The player must be able to enter a race against computer-controlled rivals. During the race the screen must show the race position, lap and time. A results screen must appear at the finish.
  • FR6 The player must be able to drive a car: accelerate, brake and reverse, steer, use the handbrake and change the camera view.
  • FR7 A crash must reduce the player’s Health and show how much Health was lost.
  • FR8 The Settings menu must let the player change the weather (Rain or Clear night) and the speed units (km/h or mph). The change must happen straight away.
  • FR9 The pause menu (Esc) must let a stuck player reset their car to the road or reset their own position.
  • FR10 Eating food from a stall must restore the player’s Health.
  • FR11 The player must be able to save their progress to a file (.json) and load it again.

8. Non-functional requirements

  • NFR1 Usability: A first-time player must be able to start a race within 3 minutes of clicking Hit the streets, without help from another person.
  • NFR2 Performance (speed of processing): The game must load in under 30 seconds and run at 30 frames per second (fps) or more on a school laptop.
  • NFR3 Reliability: The game must run for a whole lesson without crashing or freezing, and must not lose the player’s progress.
  • NFR4 Robustness: The game must cope with unexpected actions (driving into walls, going off the road, pressing keys that are not controls) without breaking.
  • NFR5 Portability: The game must run on Windows, Mac and Chromebook laptops, tablets and phones, in any modern browser, without installing anything.
  • NFR6 Maintainability: The game’s code must be easy for a developer to understand, fix and update.

E2 · Constraints: find the evidence in the finished game

A constraint limits what the developer can do. Look for features in the finished game that show how the developer worked within each constraint.

Worked example

Economic (cost, time): There is no budget to hire 3D artists, sound designers or musicians, and the game must be free to play.

Evidence in the finished game:

The Credits say the cars and characters were made with Meshy (an AI tool), the street props are free NVIDIA assets (licence CC BY 4.0) and all sounds are synthesised live in the browser. No artists or musicians had to be paid.

Why this is good evidence: it names where the evidence was found (Credits) and how it shows the developer worked within the limit.

Technical (speed of processing, capacity, equipment, compatibility, security): The game must run in a web browser on ordinary school laptops that do not have a gaming graphics card.

Where to look: Settings → Graphics; Credits (look for “simplified for the web”)

Social (the users’ level of expertise): Players range from people who have never played a driving game to experienced gamers.

Where to look: How to play; Settings → Steering assist and Gearbox; the objective line at the top of the game screen

Legal (intellectual property, ownership and privacy of data): The game must not use real car brands, logos or other people’s artwork without permission, and must not collect players’ personal information.

Where to look: Credits (licences, “logos removed”, “fictional”); Save or load a game (where is your data kept?)

Usability (accessibility, usefulness, ease of use): Players with different needs, devices and preferences must be able to play comfortably.

Where to look: Settings → Reduce motion effects, Camera shake, Invert camera Y, Speed units; the kinds of controls you can use

F3 · Effectiveness

Effectiveness measures how well the game does its job. Choose two different criteria from this list and judge the game against each one.

  • Accessibility: people with different abilities and devices can use it
  • Accuracy: numbers and information are correct (for example the lap timer and speedometer)
  • Attractiveness: it looks appealing and suits its audience
  • Clarity: it is clear what to do and what things mean
  • Communication of message: its purpose and setting come across to the player
  • Completeness: all the required features are there
  • Readability: the on-screen text is easy to read
  • Relevance: the features suit the purpose and the users
  • Timeliness: it responds quickly, and information appears when it is needed
  • Usability: it is easy to learn and use
